## II. THEORY ANALYSIS

### A. Autoregressive scan breaks lattice symmetry

We begin by identifying the architectural root cause from which all subsequent biases follow. The autoregressive Transformer NQS factorizes the full wavefunction as a product of conditional probabilities along a prescribed one-dimensional scan of the two-dimensional lattice:

$$\psi(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_N) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} f_\theta(\sigma_i | \sigma_{<i}),$$

where $\sigma_i \in \{\uparrow,\downarrow\}$ denotes the spin configuration at site $i$ in the $S^z$ basis, and the ordering $1,\ldots,N$ is defined by a snake-scan pattern that traverses the lattice row by row, alternating direction at each row boundary [1,2]. This factorization is not a mere computational convenience; it encodes a fundamental structural commitment. By imposing a one-dimensional causal order on a two-dimensional system, the ansatz explicitly breaks the point-group symmetry of the underlying Kagome lattice.

The symmetry-breaking operates through a concrete mechanism. The Kagome lattice possesses D6 rotational symmetry and a three-sublattice translational structure. The snake-scan ordering selects a single axis (the scan direction) as privileged: sites separated by one lattice constant along the scan direction are adjacent in the autoregressive chain, whereas sites separated by the identical distance perpendicular to the scan direction are separated by $O(L)$ steps in the ordering. At each row turn-back, where the scan reverses direction, adjacent physical sites on consecutive rows become maximally distant in the autoregressive sequence. This creates an artificial interface -- a "virtual one-dimensional boundary" -- that has no counterpart in the physical Hamiltonian [3].

The consequence for the wavefunction ansatz is severe. Even when the target Hamiltonian is fully translationally invariant, the autoregressive wavefunction $\psi_\theta$ is not: the conditional distribution $f_\theta(\sigma_i | \sigma_{<i})$ for sites near scan turn-backs draws on a systematically different statistical environment than for sites deep in the interior of the lattice. The causal structure inherited from the scan order means that sites near the virtual boundary receive information from a truncated neighborhood, while interior sites receive information from a neighborhood that respects the full two-dimensional connectivity. This non-uniform treatment of lattice directions is a structural property of the ansatz, not a capacity limitation -- a Transformer with sufficiently many attention heads and layers could in principle encode arbitrary long-range dependencies across the virtual boundary [4], but the conditional factorization itself breaks the symmetry at the level of the wavefunction's functional form.

The contrast with symmetry-preserving architectures is instructive. The group-equivariant convolutional neural network (GCNN) of Duric et al. [5] strictly preserves all Kagome space group symmetries by construction, including both the translational group and the full D6 point group. In such an architecture, every symmetry operation of the lattice corresponds to an exact transformation of the neural network's internal representations. The autoregressive Transformer ansatz, by contrast, has no mechanism to restore the symmetries broken by the scan ordering: the scan direction is hard-coded into the wavefunction's causal structure, and no amount of training can erase this architectural commitment. This establishes the causal root of the bias:

$$\text{Snake-scan ordering} \Rightarrow \text{Broken D6 symmetry} \Rightarrow \text{Virtual 1D boundary} \Rightarrow \text{Systematic energy bias}.$$

We emphasize that this is a proved architectural fact, not a conjecture about learning dynamics. The symmetry breaking is present regardless of network capacity, training protocol, or convergence quality. Whether its energetic consequences are numerically significant depends on the specific system under study, but its existence as a structural feature of the ansatz is unconditional.

### B. Virtual boundary free energy penalty and bias direction

Having established that the autoregressive scan introduces a virtual one-dimensional boundary, we now quantify the thermodynamic consequence of this boundary and determine the direction of the resulting systematic bias.

At each step of the autoregressive scan, the set of already-sampled sites $A = \{i: i < k\}$ is separated from the yet-unsampled sites $B = \{i: i \ge k\}$ by a one-dimensional cut $\partial A$. For a snake-scan on an $L \times L$ lattice, this cut is extensive: its length scales as $O(L)$. In a system described at low energy by a conformal field theory (CFT), a one-dimensional cut through the bulk supports edge degrees of freedom whose entropy scales logarithmically with the cut length [6,7]. The resulting free energy contribution per unit of effective temperature $T_{\text{eff}}$ (set by the finite training budget, $T_{\text{eff}} \sim 1/\sqrt{t}$ [8]) is:

$$\Delta F_{\text{virtual}} \simeq -T_{\text{eff}} \cdot \frac{c}{6} \ln L,$$

where $c$ is the central charge of the low-energy CFT describing the physical edge modes.

This free energy penalty discriminates between gapless and gapped candidate ground states. For a gapless quantum spin liquid -- characterized by algebraic correlations, a diverging correlation length $\xi \to \infty$, and an extensive low-energy density of states -- the entanglement across the virtual cut involves all length scales up to $L$, and the boundary CFT is fully gapless. The penalty therefore carries the full $\ln L$ enhancement. For a gapped state, by contrast, correlations decay exponentially with a finite correlation length $\xi \sim O(1)$, and the virtual cut excites only edge modes within a distance $\sim \xi$ of the boundary. The effective cut length contributing to the edge entropy is $O(\xi)$ rather than $O(L)$, reducing the penalty to a constant (area-law) contribution independent of system size. The free energy differential between the two classes of states is:

$$\Delta F_{\text{gapless}} - \Delta F_{\text{gapped}} \simeq -T_{\text{eff}} \cdot \frac{c}{6} \ln L + \text{const} < 0,$$

which means the autoregressive ansatz imposes a larger effective energy penalty on gapless states than on gapped states at any finite $L > 1$.

This establishes the direction of the systematic bias from statistical mechanics alone, without requiring any assumptions about learning dynamics or spectral properties of the neural tangent kernel. The virtual boundary penalty always favors states that minimize long-range entanglement across the scan cut -- i.e., gapped states -- because the logarithmic contribution to the free energy is an irreducible consequence of the one-dimensional causal structure imposed by the autoregressive factorization. The bias is therefore structurally toward gapped states, and its magnitude grows with system size as $\ln L$.

We note that this argument operates at the level of thermodynamic contributions to the effective training objective and is logically independent of the question of spectral bias in the neural tangent kernel [9,10]. The latter concerns the order in which different functional modes are learned during gradient descent and may, depending on the precise mapping between physical Fourier space and NTK eigenfunctions, favor either gapless or gapped features [2]. However, even if spectral bias were to favor the gapless state -- by, e.g., prioritizing the low-$q$ Fourier components that dominate Dirac QSL correlations -- the virtual boundary free energy penalty identified here would act in the opposite direction. Which contribution dominates in practice depends on the relative magnitudes of $T_{\text{eff}} \cdot (c/6) \ln L$ and the spectral bias differential, a question that cannot be settled by theory alone. What theory does establish is that the virtual boundary penalty provides a robust, sign-definite, and scaling-grounded mechanism that biases the autoregressive Transformer NQS toward gapped ground states, independent of the spectral bias controversy.
