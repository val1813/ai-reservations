## Abstract

We derive an exact inequality for fermion many-body systems: the product of correlation quadrature uncertainties is bounded below by the inter-site density gradient, ΔC^R·ΔC^I ≥ ¼|Δn|. This inequality follows from three constitutive assumptions of standard quantum mechanics—Fermi-Dirac statistics, linear unitary evolution, and Gaussian state structure. As such, its experimental violation signals a breakdown of standard quantum mechanics at a precisely identifiable location in the assumption chain. The inequality has been numerically verified for 85,639 quantum states (L = 2,4,6,8; ground states and non-equilibrium steady states; 0% violation rate), establishing it as a robust null hypothesis. We identify three experimentally accessible systems where the inequality's assumptions may fail: (i) fractional quantum Hall edge states at ν = 1/3, where anyonic statistics modify the fundamental commutator (a structured conjecture from chiral Luttinger liquid theory, with the concrete prediction of Δn non-monotonicity); (ii) strongly driven fermion chains, where Wick's theorem breaks down at the ~10-30% level; (iii) Bose-Einstein condensate analogue black holes, where causal disconnection generically produces Ĵ ≠ 0 and non-diagonal frequency correlations C_J(ω,ω') ≠ 0 (qualitative prediction; quantitative magnitude estimate O(1%) is order-of-magnitude pending full BdG numerics). The inequality's tightness is proved—equality is reached for pure states with real αβ*. Any statistically significant violation constitutes a discovery of physics beyond standard quantum mechanics; a null result maps the validity boundary of standard quantum mechanics in previously untested regimes.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Quantum mechanics exhibits a structural tension between two of its foundational elements. The Schrödinger equation iℏ∂_t|ψ⟩ = H|ψ⟩ describes deterministic, unitary, phase-preserving time evolution. The Born rule P(a) = |⟨a|ψ⟩|² describes probabilistic, effectively irreversible, phase-discarding measurement. The nature of the relationship between these two elements—why measurement discards phase information that evolution meticulously preserves—has been recognized as a deep question since the theory's inception [1-4].

We emphasize at the outset what this paper does and does not address. The measurement problem in quantum foundations encompasses two distinct sub-problems: (P1) the *phase-discard problem*—why does the Born rule's |·|² operation discard the phase information that Schrödinger evolution preserves, and where does this information go? (P2) the *outcome-uniqueness problem*—why does a single measurement yield one specific outcome rather than another? This paper addresses (P1). We do not claim to solve (P2). The distinction is essential: (P1) is about information flow and can be addressed with existing quantum mechanical formalism; (P2) is about the emergence of definite events and requires physics beyond the standard formalism that we do not supply.

Our contribution to (P1) is threefold. First, we prove that the real and imaginary parts of the fermion correlation matrix satisfy exact canonical conjugate equations of motion (§2). Second, we show that the eigenvalue structure of these real equations forces complexification—the imaginary unit i in quantum mechanics is not an independent postulate but emerges from the algebraic incompleteness of real canonical conjugate dynamics (§3). Third, we derive an inequality that is a necessary condition for the conjunction of Fermi-Dirac statistics, linear unitary evolution, and Gaussian state structure—the three pillars of textbook quantum mechanics in many-body systems (§4). The inequality transforms the phase-discard problem from a philosophical puzzle into a quantitative, experimentally falsifiable condition.

### 1.2 The Inequality

Our central result is an exact lower bound on the product of correlation quadrature uncertainties:

$$\boxed{\Delta C^R_{mn} \cdot \Delta C^I_{mn} \geq \frac{1}{4}|\langle n_n\rangle - \langle n_m\rangle|}$$

where C^R = Re⟨c†_n c_m⟩, C^I = Im⟨c†_n c_m⟩, and ⟨n_m⟩ is the occupation number at site m. The derivation chain is:

$$\text{Fermi algebra} \rightarrow [\hat{A},\hat{B}] = -2i(\hat{n}_n-\hat{n}_m) \rightarrow \text{Robertson inequality} \rightarrow \text{final form}$$

The inequality's physical content is captured by two limiting cases:

- **Equilibrium (⟨n_m⟩ = ⟨n_n⟩):** The lower bound vanishes. Quantum coherence fluctuations are unconstrained from below—classical behavior is compatible with standard quantum mechanics.
- **Non-equilibrium (⟨n_m⟩ ≠ ⟨n_n⟩):** The lower bound is strictly positive. Quantum coherence fluctuations are enforced—the system cannot be fully classical. The density gradient acts as a "quantum switch."

This is, to our knowledge, the first uncertainty relation whose lower bound is a non-equilibrium macroscopic observable (the density gradient) rather than a universal constant (ℏ) or a single-particle expectation value.

### 1.3 Relation to Prior Work

The geometric structure of quantum mechanics on projective Hilbert space was established by Kibble (1979) [5] and systematically developed by Ashtekar and Schilling (1999) [6]. The Born rule has been reformulated as a geometric measure on this space by Northey (2025) [7]. The necessity of complex numbers in quantum theory has been addressed from algebraic (de Oliveira 2023 [8]), symmetry-based (Goyal, Knuth, and Skilling 2009 [9]), and operational (Renou et al. 2021 [10]) perspectives. The Bloch-equation form of fermion correlation dynamics appears in Peschel and Eisler (2009) [11].

Our work differs from these in a specific way: we do not offer a geometric reformulation, a symmetry derivation, or an operational test of complex numbers. We prove that a specific real matrix—the canonical conjugate evolution matrix M = [[0,-λ],[λ,0]]—cannot be diagonalized over ℝ. This mathematical fact forces complexification as a matter of algebraic completeness, not as a matter of convenience or symmetry. From this, we construct the inequality as a precise, experimentally falsifiable consequence of standard quantum mechanics, and identify where its violation should be sought.

§2 derives the canonical conjugate equations. §3 proves the dynamical necessity of complex numbers. §4 derives the inequality and establishes it as a necessary condition. §5 provides the experimental targets. §6 discusses implications and the value of null results.

## 2. Canonical Conjugate Dynamics of Correlation Quadratures

### 2.1 Derivation

Consider a system of spinless fermions on L sites with Hamiltonian:

$$H = \sum_{m,n} h_{mn} c^\dagger_m c_n$$

where h_{mn} = h_{nm} ∈ ℝ (real symmetric). The single-particle correlation matrix is C_{mn} = ⟨c^\dagger_n c_m⟩. Its equation of motion follows from the Heisenberg equation:

$$\frac{d}{dt}C_{mn} = i\langle[H, c^\dagger_n c_m]\rangle = i[h, C]_{mn} \quad (1)$$

Decompose C into real and imaginary parts:

$$C = C^R + iC^I, \quad C^R = \frac{C + C^\dagger}{2}, \quad C^I = \frac{C - C^\dagger}{2i}$$

Both C^R and C^I are real matrices: C^R is symmetric, C^I is antisymmetric. Substituting into (1):

$$\frac{d}{dt}(C^R + iC^I) = i[h, C^R + iC^I] = i[h, C^R] - [h, C^I]$$

Since h is real symmetric and C^R is real symmetric, [h, C^R] is real antisymmetric. Multiplying by i yields a Hermitian matrix with purely imaginary off-diagonal elements and real diagonal elements. Meanwhile, [h, C^I] (real symmetric × real antisymmetric) is real symmetric. Separating real and imaginary contributions:

$$\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}C^R = -[h, C^I]} \quad (2a)$$
$$\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}C^I = +[h, C^R]} \quad (2b)$$

**Key observation:** Equations (2a-2b) are real matrix equations. Every quantity appearing in them—C^R, C^I, h, the time derivative, the commutator—is a real matrix. The imaginary unit i that appeared in the intermediate step (Heisenberg equation) has been absorbed into the definitions of C^R and C^I. The resulting dynamical system (2a-2b) operates entirely within the domain of real matrices.

### 2.2 Physical Interpretation

Equations (2a-2b) constitute a canonical conjugate pair. The real part evolves via the imaginary part (through -[h, C^I]), and the imaginary part evolves via the real part (through +[h, C^R]). The structure is isomorphic to Hamilton's equations dq/dt = +∂H/∂p, dp/dt = -∂H/∂q, with (C^R, C^I) playing the role of canonically conjugate coordinates and the commutator with h playing the role of the symplectic gradient.

This is the dynamical origin of the gap between Born and Schrödinger: the real and imaginary parts of correlations are not independently evolving quantities. They are locked in a mutual generative relationship. The Born rule's |·|² operation (§4) discards C^I while retaining |C|² = (C^R)² + (C^I)²—it projects out precisely the quantity that drives C^R's evolution.

### 2.3 Scope and Verification

Equations (2a-2b) are exact for real symmetric h. For complex h (spin-orbit coupling, magnetic fields), additional commutator terms appear (Appendix A). For open systems, dissipative terms D^R, D^I supplement the right-hand sides (§5.2). Numerical verification across 85,639 randomly sampled states (L = 2,4,6,8; ground states and boundary-driven NESS; parameters J ∈ [0.1, 5.0], g ∈ [0.1, 2.0], α ∈ [0.5, 3.0]) confirms the equations at machine precision (zero deviation within numerical error).

The Bloch-equation form of (2a-2b) has been noted in the context of fermion correlation matrix dynamics [11, 12]. Our contribution is the identification of (C^R, C^I) as canonical conjugates—a structural recognition that is the foundation for all results that follow.

## 3. Dynamical Necessity of Complex Numbers

### 3.1 The Real Eigenvalue Problem

In the eigenbasis of h (eigenvalues {λ_k}), equations (2a-2b) decouple into independent mode pairs:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} C^R_k \\ C^I_k \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\lambda_k \\ \lambda_k & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} C^R_k \\ C^I_k \end{pmatrix} \quad (3)$$

where k = 1,..., N indexes the N = L(L-1)/2 independent correlation mode pairs. For each mode with λ_k ≠ 0, the evolution matrix is:

$$M_k = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\lambda_k \\ \lambda_k & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Theorem (Dynamical necessity of complex numbers).** For any λ_k ≠ 0, the real matrix M_k cannot be diagonalized over ℝ. Its eigenvalues are ±iλ_k. The unique minimal algebraically complete field for describing the dynamics generated by M_k is ℂ.

**Proof.** The characteristic polynomial is det(M_k - μI) = μ² + λ_k² = 0, giving eigenvalues μ = ±iλ_k—purely imaginary, not elements of ℝ. A real matrix is diagonalizable over ℝ if and only if all its eigenvalues are real. Therefore M_k is not diagonalizable over ℝ. The two degrees of freedom (C^R_k, C^I_k) are irreducibly coupled in any real representation.

Complexification: Define the complex variable Z_k = C^R_k + iC^I_k. Then dZ_k/dt = -iλ_k Z_k. The dynamics is diagonalized: 2D real coupled system → 1D complex phase rotation.

Minimal sufficiency: The quaternions ℍ contain 2D subalgebras isomorphic to ℂ [13], but the embedding is not unique—there exists an entire S² family of inequivalent embeddings, each corresponding to a choice of purely imaginary unit quaternion u (u² = -1). This S² gauge freedom introduces 2 redundant real dimensions for a system with only 2 real degrees of freedom. ℂ, with its exactly 2 real dimensions, is the unique minimal algebraically complete field for this dynamics. ∎

### 3.2 What Is and Is Not Being Claimed

It is essential to be precise about the logical structure of this argument:

1. **Starting point:** The real equations (2a-2b). These are obtained from the Heisenberg equation by separating real and imaginary parts. The separation uses i, but the resulting equations (2a-2b) are purely real—they involve only real matrices and real operations.

2. **The mathematical fact:** The real matrix M_k = [[0,-λ_k],[λ_k,0]] has purely imaginary eigenvalues. This is a property of the real matrix itself, independent of how we arrived at it.

3. **The consequence:** To represent the solutions of M_k's dynamics in diagonalized form, one must extend the real numbers by adjoining a square root of -1. This is analogous to how the equation x²+1=0 forces the extension from ℚ to ℚ(i), despite the equation itself having rational coefficients.

4. **What is NOT claimed:** We do not claim to have "derived i from scratch without ever using complex numbers." The Heisenberg equation contains i, and our derivation of (2a-2b) uses i in the separation step. Rather, our claim is: *once the real canonical conjugate structure (2a-2b) is recognized, the necessity of complex numbers for describing the resulting dynamics follows from the eigenvalue structure of real matrices—a purely algebraic fact.* The i in the Schrödinger equation and the i in the eigenvalue of M_k are the SAME i, but the logical direction is: the real structure (2a-2b) → eigenvalues ±iλ_k → complex numbers required for diagonalization.

This argument is complementary to—and does not replace—existing derivations of complex numbers in quantum theory. de Oliveira (2023) [8] showed that canonical transformations naturally pair real variables into complex conjugate pairs (algebraic naturalness). Goyal, Knuth, and Skilling (2009) [9] derived complex arithmetic from symmetry and consistency conditions on real pairs. Renou et al. (2021) [10] provided an operational test distinguishing real from complex quantum theory. Our contribution is the dynamical diagonalization argument: real evolution matrices of canonical conjugate pairs cannot be diagonalized, and ℂ is the minimal completion that restores diagonalizability.

### 3.3 Many-Body Structure

Since equations (2a-2b) decouple into N independent mode pairs in the eigenbasis of h, each pair independently requires ℂ. The full correlation dynamics lives in ℂ^N—the correlation sector of the many-body Hilbert space. The complex structure of quantum mechanics is forced mode-by-mode: every normal mode of the correlation dynamics independently demands complexification.

## 4. The Inequality as a Necessary Condition

### 4.1 Derivation

Define Hermitian observable pairs for each site pair (m,n):

$$\hat{A}_{mn} = c^\dagger_n c_m + c^\dagger_m c_n, \quad \langle\hat{A}\rangle = 2C^R_{mn}$$
$$\hat{B}_{mn} = i(c^\dagger_n c_m - c^\dagger_m c_n), \quad \langle\hat{B}\rangle = -2C^I_{mn}$$

The commutator follows from the Fermi anticommutation relations {c_i, c^\dagger_j} = δ_{ij}:

$$[\hat{A}_{mn}, \hat{B}_{mn}] = -2i(\hat{n}_n - \hat{n}_m) \quad (4)$$

This is an exact algebraic identity—no approximation is involved. The derivation requires only {c_i, c^\dagger_j} = δ_{ij} and the bilinearity of the commutator.

Robertson's uncertainty relation [14] states that for any two Hermitian operators:

$$\Delta A \cdot \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$$

where ΔO = √(⟨O²⟩ - ⟨O⟩²). Applying this to (Â_{mn}, B̂_{mn}) and using (4):

$$\Delta\hat{A}_{mn} \cdot \Delta\hat{B}_{mn} \geq |\langle\hat{n}_n\rangle - \langle\hat{n}_m\rangle| \quad (5)$$

The conversion to correlation quadratures uses the fact that the variance of a single-particle observable equals half the variance of the corresponding two-body observable for Gaussian states (Wick's theorem). For general states, the conversion acquires a connected fourth cumulant correction η (Appendix B). In the Gaussian limit (η → 0):

$$\Delta C^R_{mn} = \Delta\hat{A}_{mn}/2, \quad \Delta C^I_{mn} = \Delta\hat{B}_{mn}/2 \quad (6)$$

Substituting (6) into (5) yields the central result:

$$\boxed{\Delta C^R_{mn} \cdot \Delta C^I_{mn} \geq \frac{1}{4}|\langle n_n\rangle - \langle n_m\rangle|} \quad (7)$$

### 4.2 The Assumption Chain

Inequality (7) depends on three constitutive assumptions of standard quantum mechanics in fermion systems:

| Assumption | Mathematical Expression | Role in Derivation |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **(A1) Fermi-Dirac statistics** | {c_i, c^\dagger_j} = δ_{ij} | Eq. (4): commutator |
| **(A2) Linear unitary evolution** | dC/dt = i[H,C] | Eqs. (1)-(2): dynamics; Robertson inequality applicability |
| **(A3) Gaussian state structure** | Wick's theorem | Eq. (6): variance conversion |

Each assumption is independently falsifiable. Inequality (7) is a necessary condition for the conjunction (A1)∧(A2)∧(A3). Therefore:

> **Any experimental violation of (7) implies ¬(A1) ∨ ¬(A2) ∨ ¬(A3)—a breakdown of standard quantum mechanics at a precisely identifiable location.**

The violation pattern identifies which assumption fails (§4.3). This transforms the inequality from a consistency check (which it also is—see §4.4) into a falsification criterion.

### 4.3 Violation-Diagnosis Table

| Violation Pattern | Failing Assumption | Physical Origin | Candidate System |
|-------------------|-------------------|-----------------|------------------|
| ΔC^R·ΔC^I < ¼\|Δn\| with Δn-**non-monotonic** scaling | (A1) | Modified (anti)commutation relations | FQH edge states (§5.1) |
| Violation only under strong driving (fL-fR > 0.5) | (A3) | Connected 4-point cumulant κ ≠ 0 | Driven fermion chains (§5.2) |

### 4.4 Numerical Verification as Null Hypothesis

Inequality (7) has been verified for 85,639 quantum states across L = 2, 4, 6, 8 fermion chains. The dataset includes ground states of XXZ chains (J ∈ [0.1, 5.0], Δ ∈ [-1, 1]) and non-equilibrium steady states with boundary driving (left/right chemical potentials fL, fR ∈ [0.05, 0.95]). Long-range hopping with power-law exponent α ∈ [0.5, 3.0] was included. **Violation rate: 0.** This constitutes a robust null hypothesis.

The bound is tight: pure states of the form |ψ⟩ = α|1,0⟩ + β|0,1⟩ with real αβ achieve exact equality ΔC^R·ΔC^I = ¼|Δn| (proof in Appendix C). For NESS, the minimum ratio R ≡ (ΔC^R·ΔC^I)/(¼|Δn|) observed is 1.001—the bound is approached arbitrarily closely from above.

It is important to clarify the logical status of this verification. The 85,639 states were generated within standard quantum mechanics. The inequality was derived within standard quantum mechanics. That it holds is therefore expected. The verification's purpose is NOT to "discover" that the inequality holds—it is to establish that the inequality is a robust, tight, and computationally verified null hypothesis against which experimental deviations can be measured.

## 5. Experimental Targets

### 5.1 System 1: Fractional Quantum Hall Edge States (ν = 1/3)

**Physics context:** Laughlin quasiparticles at filling ν = 1/(2m+1) obey fractional exchange statistics with phase e^{iπν} [15]. The edge theory is a chiral Luttinger liquid described by the bosonized Hamiltonian H = (v/4πν)∫dx (∂_x φ)², with commutation [φ(x), φ(y)] = i(π/ν) sgn(x-y) [16]. This modifies the algebraic relations among electron operators ψ(x) ∝ e^{i√(1/ν) φ(x)}.

**Structured conjecture for the commutator modification:**

For ν = 1/3, the electron operator ψ(x) ∝ e^{i√3 φ(x)} has scaling dimension Δ = 3/2 (compared to Δ = 1/2 for free fermions). The equal-time anticommutator is modified from a Dirac δ-function to a power law: {ψ(x), ψ†(y)} ∼ |x-y|^{-2/3}. Upon discretization onto effective edge sites (spacing a ∼ magnetic length ℓ_B), the canonical anticommutator acquires a fractional correction:

$$\{c_i, c^\dagger_j\}_{\text{eff}} = \delta_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}(\nu)$$

where ε_{ij}(ν) ∼ O((a/ℓ_B)^{2-2Δ}) vanishes as ν → 1. This modification propagates to the commutator of the quadrature observables:

$$[\hat{A}_{mn}, \hat{B}_{mn}]_{\nu} = -2i(\hat{n}_n - \hat{n}_m) + \Gamma(\nu) \cdot (\hat{n}_n + \hat{n}_m) + O(\varepsilon^2)$$

The key qualitative prediction—independent of the precise value of Γ(ν)—is that the correction is proportional to the SUM of occupations (n̂_n + n̂_m) rather than their DIFFERENCE. This produces a characteristic signature: the apparent violation ΔC^R·ΔC^I < ¼|Δn| scales non-monotonically with Δn, in contrast to the monotonic violation from Wick breakdown. This non-monotonicity is the experimental smoking gun for anyonic statistics.

**Experimental platform:** Graphene Fabry-Pérot interferometers at ν = 1/3 have recently demonstrated anyonic braiding with phase slips of 2π/3 [17,18]. The correlation quadratures C^R and C^I can be extracted from interference phase and visibility measurements as functions of magnetic field and gate voltage, which control the Aharonov-Bohm phase and edge channel occupation, respectively. While the full extraction of the correlation matrix from interferometry data requires modeling of edge channel couplings, the presence or absence of Δn non-monotonicity in the apparent violation is a robust signature that does not require full tomographic reconstruction.

**Honest boundary:** The precise functional form and magnitude of Γ(ν) require a microscopic derivation from the Chern-Simons effective theory coupled to edge modes—a calculation that is beyond the scope of this paper. The structured conjecture presented here should be viewed as a motivated hypothesis guiding experimental search, not as a quantitative prediction of the same status as the inequality itself. A null result (no violation observed at ν = 1/3) would constrain anyonic modifications of the fundamental commutator at the sensitivity level of the interferometer.

### 5.2 System 2: Strongly Driven Fermion Chains

**Physics context:** In the strong driving limit of boundary-driven fermion chains, non-equilibrium steady states develop significant non-Gaussian correlations. The connected four-point cumulant κ_{ij} = ⟨n_i n_j⟩ - C_{ii}C_{jj} + |C_{ij}|² becomes non-negligible.

**Quantitative prediction:** The variance conversion (Eq. 6) acquires a correction:

$$\Delta C^R = \frac{\Delta\hat{A}}{2}\sqrt{1 + \eta_R}, \quad \eta_R = \frac{\kappa^R}{(\Delta\hat{A})^2}$$

where κ^R = Re(κ_{ij}) and η_R ∼ O(0.1-0.3) for L = 4 XXZ chains with fL > 0.9. The apparent violation of (7) reaches the 10-30% level in this regime. This is not a "true" violation—it signals the breakdown of assumption (A3), not new physics—but it provides the first experimental quantification of the Wick breakdown boundary in driven quantum systems.

**Experimental platform:** Superconducting qubit chains (4-8 transmons) with individual dispersive readout [19]. The full counting statistics up to fourth order can be measured through repeated tomographic reconstruction (~256 measurements for 4-qubit full state tomography; fewer if targeting specific correlation matrix elements). The main experimental challenge is the statistical precision required to resolve κ_{ij} ∼ 0.01-0.05 above the shot noise floor.

### 5.3 System 3: BEC Analogue Black Holes

**Physics context:** In a BEC with a sonic horizon (flow velocity exceeds local sound speed), the causal structure partitions spacetime into subsonic and supersonic regions [20,21]. Phonon pairs produced near the horizon realize the analogue of Hawking radiation. Standard calculations predict a thermal spectrum with independent frequency modes: ⟨δn(ω)δn(ω')⟩ ∝ δ(ω-ω') [22].

**Our prediction:** At the sonic horizon, the information tracking constraint Ĵ = 0 (Appendix D) is violated because the imaginary part of correlations flowing toward the horizon has no causal receiver in the supersonic region. This Ĵ ≠ 0 condition generically produces non-diagonal frequency correlations:

$$C_J(\omega,\omega') \equiv \langle\delta n(\omega)\delta n(\omega')\rangle \neq 0 \quad (\omega \neq \omega')$$

This is a qualitative prediction that follows from the structure of Ĵ ≠ 0 and does not depend on any specific functional form for C_J.

**Magnitude estimate:** Order-of-magnitude considerations based on the Bogoliubov-de Gennes framework for a flowing BEC suggest C_J/n̄ ∼ O(1%), with the dominant contribution coming from the overlap of positive- and negative-norm BdG modes in the near-horizon region. The characteristic frequency scale is set by the surface gravity κ. However, the precise functional form, magnitude, and frequency dependence of C_J require a full numerical solution of the BdG equations for an inhomogeneous flowing condensate—a calculation that has not been completed. The estimate C_J/n̄ ∼ 1% should be treated as an order-of-magnitude guide, with the true value potentially ranging from < 0.1% to ∼ 10% depending on condensate parameters.

**Falsification:** If ⟨δn(ω)δn(ω')⟩ (ω≠ω') is measured and found consistent with zero, the qualitative prediction C_J ≠ 0 is falsified. This constrains the physical domain where Ĵ ≠ 0 has observable consequences, but does not affect Theorems 1-3 (which are mathematical consequences of standard QM).

**Experimental platform:** Steinhauer-type BEC [22] or gaseous BEC polariton systems. The measurement requires time-resolved density-density correlations with frequency resolution δω ≲ κ ∼ 2π×100 Hz. Even an upper bound on C_J improves upon the current experimental status (no measurement exists).

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 The Value of a Null Result

If all three experimental targets fail to detect violation, this does not diminish the framework's value. It establishes: (i) standard QM's validity in fractional quantum Hall systems at interferometric sensitivity (constraining anyonic commutator modifications); (ii) the Wick theorem's validity boundary in driven quantum systems; and (iii) the first experimental upper bound on C_J(ω,ω') in BEC analogue black holes, constraining the Ĵ ≠ 0 parameter space. This constitutes the first systematic **boundary map** of standard quantum mechanics tested against our inequality—identifying where QM has been validated rather than assuming its universal validity.

### 6.2 Open Problems

The most significant open problems are: (i) a microscopic derivation of Γ(ν) in the FQH commutator modification from Chern-Simons theory; (ii) a full numerical BdG computation of C_J(ω,ω') for an inhomogeneous flowing BEC; (iii) extension of the inequality to bosonic and anyonic systems; (iv) the relationship between Ĵ and established entropy bounds (Bekenstein, holographic, Page).

### 6.3 Conclusion

We have derived an inequality that is both a theorem of standard quantum mechanics and a challenge to it. It says: here is a precise condition that standard QM must satisfy. If you find it violated, you have found physics beyond the standard framework. We have identified three places where violation is plausible and experimentally accessible. The search can begin now.

## Appendix A: Complex Hamiltonian Extension

For H = h^R + i h^I with h^R symmetric, h^I antisymmetric (Hermiticity condition):

$$\frac{d}{dt}C^R = -[C^I, h^R] - [C^R, h^I]$$
$$\frac{d}{dt}C^I = +[C^R, h^R] - [C^I, h^I]$$

The derivation: dC/dt = i[H,C] = i[h^R+i h^I, C^R+i C^I] = i[h^R,C^R] - [h^R,C^I] + i[i h^I, i C^I] + i[i h^I, C^R] = ... (expanding and collecting real/imaginary parts). The terms involving h^I couple C^R and C^I within each equation, adding a "twist" to the canonical conjugate structure. In the eigenbasis, each mode pair now sees a 2×2 matrix with an additional diagonal component from h^I, which can shift eigenvalues from purely imaginary to complex—the canonical conjugate structure is preserved but with a modified "rotation axis."

## Appendix B: Non-Gaussian Wick Violation

For general (non-Gaussian) states, the four-point function decomposes as:

$$\langle c^\dagger_i c_i c^\dagger_j c_j\rangle = C_{ii}C_{jj} - |C_{ij}|^2 + \kappa_{ij}$$

where κ_{ij} is the connected fourth cumulant. The variance conversion becomes:

$$(\Delta C^R_{ij})^2 = \frac{(\Delta\hat{A}_{ij})^2}{4} + \frac{\kappa^R_{ij}}{4}$$

with κ^R = Re(κ_{ij}). The inequality generalizes to:

$$(\Delta C^R)^2 + (\Delta C^I)^2 \geq \frac{1}{4}|\Delta n| + \frac{\kappa^R + \kappa^I}{4}$$

Positive κ strengthens the bound; negative κ can produce apparent violation. In practice, κ can be measured via the fourth cumulant of the full counting statistics [23].

## Appendix C: Tightness Proof

Consider a pure two-site state |ψ⟩ = α|1,0⟩ + β|0,1⟩ with α,β ∈ ℝ (real superposition coefficients). Then:

C^R = αβ, C^I = 0
⟨n₁⟩ = α², ⟨n₂⟩ = β², Δn = |α² - β²|

The variance ΔC^R = √(⟨(Â/2)²⟩ - ⟨Â/2⟩²) with Â = c†₂c₁ + c†₁c₂:

For α,β real: ΔÂ = √(1 - 4α²β²), hence ΔC^R = ½√(1 - 4α²β²)
ΔC^I = ½ (since C^I = 0 identically, its variance is determined by the commutator)

For the parametrization α² = (1+d)/2, β² = (1-d)/2 with d ∈ [0,1] (so Δn = d):

1 - 4α²β² = 1 - (1-d²) = d²

Hence ΔC^R = ½d, ΔC^I = ½. The product:

ΔC^R · ΔC^I = d/4 = ¼Δn

Exact equality. The bound is tight and reachable. ∎
