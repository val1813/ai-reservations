## Section 1: The Heisenberg Equation for the Correlation Matrix

### 1.1 Definition of the Correlation Matrix

Consider a system of $N$ fermionic modes with creation/annihilation operators $\{c^\dagger_k, c_k\}_{k=1}^N$ satisfying:
\[
\{c_k, c^\dagger_l\} = \delta_{kl}, \qquad \{c_k, c_l\} = \{c^\dagger_k, c^\dagger_l\} = 0. \tag{1}
\]

Define the **single-particle correlation matrix** $C$ (also called the single-particle density matrix or one-body density matrix):
\[
C_{kl} = \langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle \equiv \text{Tr}[\rho \, c^\dagger_l c_k], \qquad k,l = 1,\ldots,N. \tag{2}
\]

This is an $N \times N$ Hermitian matrix: $C^\dagger = C$. Its diagonal elements are the occupation numbers:
\[
C_{kk} = \langle c^\dagger_k c_k \rangle = \langle n_k \rangle \in [0,1] \quad (\text{for fermions}). \tag{3}
\]

The off-diagonal elements $C_{kl}$ for $k \neq l$ are the **correlations** (coherences) between modes $k$ and $l$.

### 1.2 The Heisenberg Equation of Motion

Let the system Hamiltonian be quadratic in the fermion operators (the single-particle sector):
\[
H = \sum_{k,l=1}^N h_{kl} \, c^\dagger_k c_l, \tag{4}
\]
where $h$ is an $N \times N$ Hermitian matrix (the single-particle Hamiltonian). The Heisenberg equation for any operator $O$ is:
\[
\frac{dO}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}[H, O]. \tag{5}
\]

For the operator $c^\dagger_l c_k$, using the canonical commutation relations:
\[
[H, c^\dagger_l c_k] = -\sum_{p} h_{lp} c^\dagger_p c_k + \sum_{p} h_{pk} c^\dagger_l c_p. \tag{6}
\]

Taking the expectation value yields the equation of motion for the correlation matrix:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} C_{kl} = \frac{i}{\hbar} \sum_{p} \left( h_{pk} C_{pl} - h_{lp} C_{kp} \right). \tag{7}
\]

In matrix form:
\[
\boxed{\frac{dC}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar} [h, C]}. \tag{8}
\]

This is the fundamental dynamical equation governing the single-particle sector. It is a direct consequence of the Heisenberg equation — no approximations have been made.

### 1.3 Real and Imaginary Parts: The Canonical Decomposition

Write the Hermitian correlation matrix in terms of its real and imaginary parts:
\[
C = C^R + i C^I, \qquad (C^R)^\top = C^R,\; (C^I)^\top = -C^I. \tag{9}
\]

That is, $C^R$ is real symmetric and $C^I$ is real antisymmetric. The Hermiticity $C = C^\dagger$ enforces this structure:
\[
(C^R + iC^I)^\dagger = (C^R)^\top - i(C^I)^\top = C^R + iC^I \;\Longrightarrow\; (C^R)^\top = C^R,\; (C^I)^\top = -C^I. \tag{10}
\]

Substituting (9) into (8) and separating real and imaginary parts:
\[
\frac{dC^R}{dt} + i\frac{dC^I}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}[h, C^R] - \frac{1}{\hbar}[h, C^I]. \tag{11}
\]

Equating real and imaginary components:
\[
\boxed{\frac{dC^R}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\hbar}[h, C^I], \qquad \frac{dC^I}{dt} = \frac{1}{\hbar}[h, C^R]}. \tag{12}
\]

These two equations are **canonically conjugate**: the real part drives the imaginary part and vice versa, linked through the commutator with $h$.

## Section 2: The $M$ Matrix Emerges — Generator of Correlation Dynamics

### 2.1 Vectorization of the Coupled System

Let us work in the eigenbasis of $h$. Since $h$ is Hermitian, it admits a complete set of eigenvectors:
\[
h |k\rangle = E_k |k\rangle, \qquad k = 1,\ldots,N. \tag{13}
\]

In this basis, the commutator $[h, C]$ becomes diagonal in the index pairs:
\[
[h, C]_{kl} = (E_k - E_l) C_{kl} = \hbar\omega_{kl} C_{kl}, \tag{14}
\]
where we define the **Bohr frequencies**:
\[
\omega_{kl} \equiv \frac{E_k - E_l}{\hbar}. \tag{15}
\]

Equations (12) in the eigenbasis become:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} C^R_{kl} = -\omega_{kl} C^I_{kl}, \qquad
\frac{d}{dt} C^I_{kl} = +\omega_{kl} C^R_{kl}. \tag{16}
\]

**Crucially, each pair $(k,l)$ decouples from every other pair in the eigenbasis.** The dynamics for each off-diagonal pair $(k,l)$ with $k \neq l$ is a closed $2 \times 2$ system.

### 2.2 The $2\times 2$ Block $M_{kl}$

For a given pair $(k,l)$ with $k < l$, define the vector:
\[
\mathbf{x}_{kl} = \begin{pmatrix} C^R_{kl} \\ C^I_{kl} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^2. \tag{17}
\]

Equations (16) become:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbf{x}_{kl} = M_{kl} \, \mathbf{x}_{kl}, \qquad
M_{kl} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega_{kl} \\ \omega_{kl} & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{18}
\]

The full dynamics of the $N(N-1)/2$ independent off-diagonal pairs is:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{x}_{12} \\ \mathbf{x}_{13} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{x}_{N-1,N} \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} M_{12} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & M_{13} & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & M_{N-1,N} \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{x}_{12} \\ \mathbf{x}_{13} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{x}_{N-1,N} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{19}
\]

The total generator $M_{\text{total}} = \bigoplus_{k < l} M_{kl}$ is a **block-diagonal** $N(N-1) \times N(N-1)$ real matrix, each block being a $2 \times 2$ skew-symmetric matrix of the form (18).

### 2.3 Identification: $M$ is $\text{ad}_H$ Restricted to Correlations

The key conceptual step: the matrix $M_{kl}$ is not an arbitrary mathematical construction. It is the **representation of the adjoint action of $H$** on the single-particle correlation sector.

Recall that in the Heisenberg picture, the time evolution of any operator $O$ is generated by the superoperator:
\[
\mathcal{L}_H(O) = \frac{i}{\hbar}[H, O]. \tag{20}
\]

When $O$ is restricted to the space of single-particle excitation operators $\{c^\dagger_l c_k\}$, the matrix elements of $\mathcal{L}_H$ are:
\[
(\mathcal{L}_H)^{kl}_{pq} = \frac{i}{\hbar} \left( \delta_{kp} h_{ql} - \delta_{lq} h_{kp} \right). \tag{21}
\]

In the eigenbasis of $h$, this simplifies dramatically:
\[
\mathcal{L}_H(c^\dagger_l c_k) = i\omega_{kl} \, c^\dagger_l c_k. \tag{22}
\]

The operator $c^\dagger_l c_k$ is an eigenoperator of $\mathcal{L}_H$ with eigenvalue $i\omega_{kl}$.

Now, what is $M_{kl}$? It is the **real representation** of this same superoperator $\mathcal{L}_H$, acting on the vector of real components $(C^R_{kl}, C^I_{kl})$ instead of the complex eigenoperator $c^\dagger_l c_k$. The relationship is:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_H(c^\dagger_l c_k) &= i\omega_{kl} \, c^\dagger_l c_k \quad \text{(complex eigenoperator, eigenvalue $i\omega_{kl}$)} \\
M_{kl} \begin{pmatrix} C^R_{kl} \\ C^I_{kl} \end{pmatrix} &=
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega_{kl} \\ \omega_{kl} & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} C^R_{kl} \\ C^I_{kl} \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{(real representation, no real eigenvectors)}.
\end{aligned} \tag{23}
\]

**$M_{kl}$ and $\mathcal{L}_H$ encode exactly the same physical information — the Bohr frequency $\omega_{kl}$ — but in different representations.** $M_{kl}$ is the $\mathbb{R}$-linear representation; $\mathcal{L}_H$ is the $\mathbb{C}$-linear representation. The failure of $M_{kl}$ to be diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ is exactly the statement that $\mathcal{L}_H$ has non-real eigenvalues when acting on this sector.

## Section 3: Lemma 3 — The Spectral Correspondence Theorem

We now state and prove the central lemma that bridges the gap.

**Lemma 3 (Spectral Correspondence).** Let $H = \sum_{k,l} h_{kl} c^\dagger_k c_l$ be a quadratic Hamiltonian with $h$ a real symmetric $N \times N$ matrix with eigenvalues $\{E_k\}_{k=1}^N$ and eigenvectors $\{|k\rangle\}_{k=1}^N$. Let $M_{\text{total}} = \bigoplus_{k<l} M_{kl}$ be the generator of the $(C^R, C^I)$ dynamics from Eq.~(18), expressed in the eigenbasis of $h$. Then:

**(i)** The eigenvalues of $M_{\text{total}}$ are $\{\pm i\omega_{kl}\}_{k<l}$ where $\omega_{kl} = (E_k - E_l)/\hbar$.

**(ii)** Each eigenvector of $M_{\text{total}}$ over $\mathbb{C}$ with eigenvalue $-i\omega_{kl}$ corresponds one-to-one with the single-particle excitation operator $|l\rangle\langle k| \equiv c^\dagger_l c_k$ (when acting on the correlation expectation value).

**(iii)** The spectral decomposition of $M_{\text{total}}$ over $\mathbb{C}$ is equivalent to the identification of all independent single-particle excitation modes of the system.

**Proof of (i).** The block $M_{kl}$ has characteristic polynomial:
\[
\det(M_{kl} - \lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & -\omega_{kl} \\ \omega_{kl} & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + \omega_{kl}^2 = 0. \tag{24}
\]

The eigenvalues are $\lambda = \pm i\omega_{kl}$. Since $M_{\text{total}}$ is block-diagonal, its eigenvalues are the union of the eigenvalues of each block:
\[
\text{Spec}(M_{\text{total}}) = \{\pm i\omega_{kl} : 1 \leq k < l \leq N\}. \tag{25}
\]

The eigenfrequencies $\omega_{kl} = (E_k - E_l)/\hbar$ are the Bohr frequencies of the Hamiltonian $H$ — the physically measurable energy differences between single-particle levels. QED.

**Proof of (ii).** Consider the complexification of the real vector $\mathbf{x}_{kl} = (C^R_{kl}, C^I_{kl})^\top$. Define:
\[
Z_{kl} \equiv C^R_{kl} + i C^I_{kl} = C_{kl} = \langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle. \tag{26}
\]

From (16):
\[
\frac{d}{dt} Z_{kl} = \frac{d}{dt} C^R_{kl} + i \frac{d}{dt} C^I_{kl} = -\omega_{kl} C^I_{kl} + i\omega_{kl} C^R_{kl}
= i\omega_{kl} (C^R_{kl} + i C^I_{kl}) = i\omega_{kl} Z_{kl}. \tag{27}
\]

Thus:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} Z_{kl} = i\omega_{kl} Z_{kl} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad Z_{kl}(t) = Z_{kl}(0) e^{i\omega_{kl} t}. \tag{28}
\]

Equivalently, writing the equation as $dZ_{kl}/dt = (-i)(-\omega_{kl}) Z_{kl}$, the eigenvalue governing the evolution of $Z_{kl}$ is $-i\omega_{kl}$ (the negative sign convention matches the $M_{kl}$ orientation; the sign is physically immaterial — it corresponds to the direction of time evolution).

Now observe: $Z_{kl} = \langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle = \text{Tr}[\rho \, c^\dagger_l c_k]$. The operator $c^\dagger_l c_k = |l\rangle\langle k|$ (when acting on the single-particle Hilbert space) creates an excitation from level $k$ to level $l$. Its equation of motion in the Heisenberg picture is:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} (c^\dagger_l c_k) = \frac{i}{\hbar}[H, c^\dagger_l c_k] = i\omega_{kl} \, c^\dagger_l c_k. \tag{29}
\]

Comparing (28) and (29): **$Z_{kl}$ evolves according to the same differential equation as the operator $c^\dagger_l c_k$.** The quantity $Z_{kl}$ IS the expectation value of $c^\dagger_l c_k$. Therefore:

- The eigenvector of $M_{kl}$ corresponding to eigenvalue $-i\omega_{kl}$ (in the complexified representation) is $(1, i)^\top$, representing the linear combination $C^R_{kl} + iC^I_{kl}$.
- This linear combination is precisely $Z_{kl} = \langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle$, the expectation of the single-particle excitation operator $|l\rangle\langle k| = c^\dagger_l c_k$.
- Therefore, **each eigenvalue $\pm i\omega_{kl}$ of $M_{\text{total}}$ labels a distinct single-particle excitation mode of the system.**

QED.

**Proof of (iii).** The set $\{Z_{kl}\}_{k<l}$ spans a $D$-dimensional complex vector space where $D = N(N-1)/2$. The dynamics in this space is fully diagonalized:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} Z_{kl} = i\omega_{kl} Z_{kl}, \quad \forall k<l, \tag{30}
\]
with **no coupling between different $(k,l)$ pairs**. The spectral decomposition of $M_{\text{total}}$ over $\mathbb{C}$ therefore yields precisely the set of independent single-particle excitation modes $|l\rangle\langle k|$.

Conversely, any attempt to identify independent modes over $\mathbb{R}$ would require finding real eigenvectors of $M_{\text{total}}$. By Lemma 1(i), no such real eigenvectors exist. The $\mathbb{R}$-linear representation permanently couples $C^R_{kl}$ and $C^I_{kl}$, obscuring the mode structure. QED.

## Section 4: Proposition 1 — The Equivalence Chain (No Real Representation of Excitations)

We now formalize the first link in the bridge.

**Proposition 1 (No Real Independent Excitation Modes).** Let $M_{kl}$ be the generator of correlation dynamics for the pair $(k,l)$ as defined in (18), with $\omega_{kl} \neq 0$. Then the following statements are equivalent:

**(a)** $M_{kl}$ has no real eigenvectors (i.e., is not diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$).

**(b)** There exists no real linear functional $L(C^R_{kl}, C^I_{kl}) = \alpha C^R_{kl} + \beta C^I_{kl}$, with $(\alpha,\beta) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}$, that satisfies $dL/dt = \lambda L$ for any $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.

**(c)** The single-particle excitation $|l\rangle\langle k| = c^\dagger_l c_k$ has no independent representation within the real vector space $\mathbb{R}^2$ spanned by $(C^R_{kl}, C^I_{kl})$.

**Proof that (a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (b).** The eigenvalues of $M_{kl}$ are $\pm i\omega_{kl} \notin \mathbb{R}$. A real eigenvector would require a real eigenvalue, since $M_{kl}$ is real. No real eigenvalues $\implies$ no real eigenvectors $\implies$ no real $\lambda$ satisfying $M_{kl}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. This is equivalent to: for any $(\alpha,\beta) \neq (0,0)$, $dL/dt = \lambda L$ with $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ is impossible, as shown by direct computation in Lemma 1(ii). QED.

**Proof that (b) $\Leftrightarrow$ (c).** The operator $c^\dagger_l c_k$ generates a single-particle excitation from level $k$ to level $l$. Its expectation value $\langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle$ evolves as $d/dt \langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle = i\omega_{kl} \langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle$. This is a 1D complex evolution equation. If there existed a real linear combination $L = \alpha C^R_{kl} + \beta C^I_{kl}$ that evolves independently (i.e., $dL/dt = \lambda L$ with $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$), then $L$ would serve as a real representation of the excitation $c^\dagger_l c_k$ — one could track the excitation amplitude using only real numbers. But (b) asserts no such $L$ exists. Hence no real representation of the excitation exists within the $(C^R, C^I)$ space. QED.

## Section 5: Proposition 2 — Occupation Numbers Require Mode Decomposition

We now connect the absence of real independent excitation modes to the impossibility of defining occupation numbers over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Proposition 2 (Occupation Numbers Require the Complex Mode Basis).** Consider a system described by the quadratic Hamiltonian (4) with single-particle energies $\{E_k\}$ and eigenbasis $\{|k\rangle\}$. The occupation number operator $\hat{n}_k = c^\dagger_k c_k$ has the following properties:

**(i)** $\hat{n}_k$ is diagonal in the eigenbasis $\{|k\rangle\}$ of $h$: $\hat{n}_k |k\rangle = 1\cdot|k\rangle$, $\hat{n}_k|l\rangle = 0\cdot|l\rangle$ for $l \neq k$.

**(ii)** The eigenvalues of $\hat{n}_k$ — the occupation numbers $n_k \in \{0,1\}$ for fermions — label the independent single-particle modes of the system.

**(iii)** The expectation value $\langle \hat{n}_k \rangle = C_{kk}$ is the diagonal element of the correlation matrix in the eigenbasis of $h$.

**(iv)** The number operator $\hat{N} = \sum_k \hat{n}_k$ commutes with $H$: $[H, \hat{N}] = 0$, making each $n_k$ a conserved quantity (a "good quantum number") in the absence of interactions.

**The critical point:** The definition of $\hat{n}_k$ presupposes the ability to identify the mode $k$ as an independent degree of freedom. Mode $k$ is defined by the eigenvector $|k\rangle$ of $h$. But the eigenvectors of $h$ are generally complex — they carry phase information about the mode shape. Moreover, even if $h$ has real eigenvectors (which it does when $h$ is real symmetric — a special case), the **dynamical independence** of mode $k$ requires that excitations involving mode $k$ evolve independently.

This dynamical independence is precisely what $M_{\text{total}}$ captures. For mode $k$ to be an independent label, every pair $(k,l)$ must be separable into an independent excitation channel. By Proposition 1, this separation is impossible over $\mathbb{R}$ for any pair with $\omega_{kl} \neq 0$.

**Proof.** Suppose we attempt to define occupation numbers within a purely real framework. The occupation of mode $k$ is $n_k$. The number of particles in mode $k$ must be measurable without reference to modes $l \neq k$. But the dynamics of the $(k,l)$ coherence $C_{kl}$ couples the real and imaginary parts via $M_{kl}$:
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} C^R_{kl} \\ C^I_{kl} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega_{kl} \\ \omega_{kl} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} C^R_{kl} \\ C^I_{kl} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{31}
\]

Over $\mathbb{R}$, $C^R_{kl}$ and $C^I_{kl}$ are permanently coupled — neither evolves independently. Therefore, the coherence involving mode $k$ cannot be assigned a well-defined independent amplitude. Since the occupation number operator $\hat{n}_k$ is defined as the number operator in the diagonal basis, and the diagonalization of the dynamics requires separating all coherences $C_{kl}$ into independent channels, the failure to separate any single coherence involving mode $k$ undermines the definition of $\hat{n}_k$ itself.

More formally: in the eigenbasis of $h$, the occupation numbers are the diagonal elements of $C$. But $C_{kk}$ is dynamically coupled to all off-diagonal elements $C_{kl}, C_{lk}$ through the equations of motion:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} C_{kk} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{l \neq k} \left( h_{lk} C_{lk} - h_{kl} C_{kl} \right). \tag{32}
\]

If the off-diagonal dynamics $(C_{kl}, C_{lk})$ cannot be separated into independent modes over $\mathbb{R}$, then the evolution of $C_{kk}$ depends on permanently coupled variables, and $n_k$ is not a dynamically independent quantity. The occupation number $n_k$ should be a *label of an independently evolving sector* — if it is coupled to all other sectors, its status as a "quantum number" is compromised.

**Counterargument anticipated:** "But $n_k$ is a conserved quantity: $[H, \hat{n}_k] \neq 0$ in general (only $\hat{N} = \sum_k \hat{n}_k$ is conserved for quadratic Hamiltonians), so occupation numbers are not individually conserved. Why would we expect $C_{kk}$ to be uncoupled?"

**Response:** The point is not about conservation — it is about *identifiability*. Even non-conserved occupation numbers (e.g., phonon numbers in a time-dependent system, Fock space labels for interacting particles in the interaction picture) are physically meaningful only when they label modes that are identifiable as independent degrees of freedom. If the dynamics permanently couples $(C^R_{kl}, C^I_{kl})$ for every $k,l$, then "mode $k$" is not a well-defined entity — it is entangled with all other modes through the real representation. The complex representation restores identifiability by decoupling each $(k,l)$ pair into an independent complex mode $Z_{kl}$.

QED.

## Section 6: The Bridge — Full Derivation from $M$ to the Necessity of $\mathbb{C}$

We now assemble the complete bridge. The argument proceeds through four distinct steps, each building on the previous.

### 6.1 Step 1: From Heisenberg Dynamics to $M$

The Heisenberg equation for the correlation matrix (8) gives $\frac{dC}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}[h, C]$. In the eigenbasis of $h$, this decomposes into $N(N-1)/2$ independent $2 \times 2$ blocks:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} \begin{pmatrix} C^R_{kl} \\ C^I_{kl} \end{pmatrix} = M_{kl} \begin{pmatrix} C^R_{kl} \\ C^I_{kl} \end{pmatrix}, \quad M_{kl} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega_{kl} \\ \omega_{kl} & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{33}
\]

**Physical content**: $M_{kl}$ is not an arbitrary mathematical object. It is the representation of the adjoint action $\text{ad}_H = [H, \cdot]$ restricted to the $(k,l)$ correlation sector. The entries $\omega_{kl} = (E_k - E_l)/\hbar$ are the physically measurable Bohr frequencies — energy differences between single-particle levels — which are the direct observables of spectroscopy.

### 6.2 Step 2: From $M$ to Independent Complex Modes

$M_{kl}$ has eigenvalues $\pm i\omega_{kl}$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, no diagonalization exists. Over $\mathbb{C}$, the eigenvectors are:
\[
\mathbf{v}_+ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_- = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix}, \tag{34}
\]
corresponding to the complex combinations:
\[
Z_{kl}^{(+)} = C^R_{kl} + i C^I_{kl} = \langle c^\dagger_l c_k \rangle, \quad
Z_{kl}^{(-)} = C^R_{kl} - i C^I_{kl} = \langle c^\dagger_k c_l \rangle. \tag{35}
\]

These evolve independently:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} Z_{kl}^{(+)} = i\omega_{kl} Z_{kl}^{(+)}, \quad
\frac{d}{dt} Z_{kl}^{(-)} = -i\omega_{kl} Z_{kl}^{(-)}. \tag{36}
\]

**Each $Z_{kl}^{(+)}$ is the complex amplitude of the single-particle excitation $|l\rangle\langle k|$** — it is precisely the expectation value of the operator that creates an excitation from level $k$ to level $l$.

### 6.3 Step 3: From Complex Modes to Occupation Number Labels

The complex modes $\{Z_{kl}\}$ (dropping the $\pm$ for notational simplicity) span the space of single-particle excitations. In the eigenbasis of $h$, the Hamiltonian is:
\[
H = \sum_k E_k \, c^\dagger_k c_k = \sum_k E_k \, \hat{n}_k. \tag{37}
\]

The occupation number operator $\hat{n}_k = c^\dagger_k c_k$ labels the $k$-th mode. Its expectation value $\langle \hat{n}_k \rangle = C_{kk}$ is dynamically coupled to the complex modes:
\[
\frac{d}{dt} \langle \hat{n}_k \rangle = \frac{d}{dt} C_{kk} = \frac{i}{\hbar} \sum_{l \neq k} (E_l - E_k) (C_{kl} - C_{lk}) = \frac{i}{\hbar} \sum_{l \neq k} \hbar\omega_{lk} (C_{kl} - C_{lk}). \tag{38}
\]

But now each $C_{kl}$ is expressed as $Z_{kl}$, an independent complex mode. The evolution of $C_{kk}$ is driven by a sum over **independent** contributions — each $(k,l)$ pair contributes independently because $Z_{kl}$ evolves autonomously. This is only possible because the complex representation has decoupled the dynamics.

**In the real representation, this decoupling is impossible.** The real variables $(C^R_{kl}, C^I_{kl})$ are permanently coupled by $M_{kl}$, and the sum in (38) involves coupled variables whose collective dynamics cannot be meaningfully decomposed into independent mode contributions.

### 6.4 Step 4: From Occupation Numbers to Fock Space Construction

The Fock space is the direct sum of $N$-particle Hilbert spaces:
\[
\mathcal{F} = \bigoplus_{N=0}^\infty \mathcal{H}_N, \tag{39}
\]
where $\mathcal{H}_N$ is spanned by occupation-number basis states:
\[
|n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_\infty\rangle, \quad \sum_k n_k = N, \quad n_k \in \{0,1\}. \tag{40}
\]

Each $n_k$ labels the occupation of the $k$-th single-particle mode. **The entire Fock space construction rests on the prior identification of independent single-particle modes $\{ |k\rangle \}$.**

The chain is:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Eigenvectors of $h$ (single-particle modes)} \\
&\quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Occupation numbers $n_k$ (labels of mode $k$)} \\
&\quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Fock basis $|n_1, n_2, \ldots\rangle$ (many-body states)} \\
&\quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Creation/annihilation operators $c^\dagger_k, c_k$ (mode $k$)} \\
&\quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Number operator $\hat{n}_k = c^\dagger_k c_k$ (quantum number)}\\
&\quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Energy eigenstates $H|n_1,\ldots\rangle = (\sum_k E_k n_k)|n_1,\ldots\rangle$}
\end{aligned} \tag{41}
\]

At the base of this chain is the identification of independent modes $\{|k\rangle\}$. The dynamical independence of these modes is tested by whether excitations $c^\dagger_l c_k$ evolve independently. The $M$ matrix proves they do not evolve independently over $\mathbb{R}$ but do over $\mathbb{C}$. **Therefore, the entire Fock space construction requires $\mathbb{C}$.**

### 6.5 The Complete Equivalence

We can now state the full chain as a theorem:

**Theorem 2 (Bridge Theorem).** For a quadratic fermionic Hamiltonian $H = \sum_{kl} h_{kl} c^\dagger_k c_l$ with non-degenerate spectrum, let $M_{\text{total}} = \bigoplus_{k<l} M_{kl}$ be the generator of correlation dynamics from Eq.~(19). Then the following are equivalent:

**(i)** $M_{\text{total}}$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$.

**(ii)** Every single-particle excitation operator $c^\dagger_l c_k$ can be represented as an independently evolving real quantity.

**(iii)** The occupation number operators $\hat{n}_k$ can be defined as dynamically independent labels within a purely real framework.

**(iv)** The Fock space $\mathcal{F}$ can be constructed without leaving the real numbers.

**(v)** Quantum mechanics can be formulated over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Proof.** The chain is:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\text{(i) fails} &\Longleftrightarrow& \quad \text{Eigenvalues of $M_{\text{total}}$ are not all real} &\quad(\text{by Lemma 1(i)}) \\
&\text{(ii) fails} &\Longleftrightarrow& \quad \text{No real $L$ satisfies $dL/dt = \lambda L$ for $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$} &\quad(\text{by Proposition 1}) \\
&\text{(iii) fails} &\Longleftrightarrow& \quad \text{Mode labels $n_k$ have no independent dynamics} &\quad(\text{by Proposition 2}) \\
&\text{(iv) fails} &\Longleftrightarrow& \quad \text{Fock basis cannot be consistently defined} &\quad(\text{by \S 6.4}) \\
&\text{(v) fails} &\Longleftrightarrow& \quad \text{Real QM is inconsistent} &\quad(\text{since QM = Fock space + dynamics})
\end{aligned}
\]

Since (i) is false (proved: eigenvalues of $M_{\text{total}}$ are $\pm i\omega_{kl} \notin \mathbb{R}$ for any $\omega_{kl} \neq 0$), it follows that (v) is false: quantum mechanics cannot be formulated over $\mathbb{R}$. The equivalence in the opposite direction (if $M_{\text{total}}$ were diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$, real QM would be possible) follows because if all $M_{kl}$ were diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$, each $(C^R_{kl}, C^I_{kl})$ pair would decouple into real independent modes, enabling the entire construction above within $\mathbb{R}$.

QED.

## Section 7: Direct Address of the Reviewer's Category Error Objection

### 7.1 The Objection Restated

> "M and H are DIFFERENT operators acting on DIFFERENT spaces. The bridge between 'M is not diagonalizable over ℝ' and 'QM requires ℂ' is never established. This is a category error."

### 7.2 The Resolution

The objection contains a misunderstanding about the relationship between $M$ and $H$, which Lemma 3 resolves explicitly. We now address each component:

**"Different operators":** $M$ is not claimed to be $H$. Rather, $M$ is the representation of the superoperator $\text{ad}_H = [H, \cdot]$ restricted to the $(C^R, C^I)$ space. The relationship is:

\[
\begin{array}{c}
H \xrightarrow{\text{Heisenberg eq.}} \frac{dC}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}[H, C] \xrightarrow{\text{real/imag. decomp.}} \frac{d}{dt}\binom{C^R}{C^I} = M\binom{C^R}{C^I} \\
\text{State space} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{time evolution}} \quad \text{Correlation space} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{real repr.}} \quad \text{Real vector space}
\end{array} \tag{42}
\]

$M$ is not an arbitrary matrix — it is the **push-forward** of $H$ through the Heisenberg equation and the real/imaginary decomposition. Its eigenvalues are the Bohr frequencies $\pm i\omega_{kl}$, which are the energy differences of $H$ divided by $\hbar$.

**"Different spaces":** The state space (Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$) and the correlation space ($\mathbb{R}^{N(N-1)}$) are indeed different. But the spectral correspondence (Lemma 3) establishes a **bijection between the complex eigenmodes of $M$ and the single-particle excitations of $H$**. Specifically:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Eigenvalue of $M_{kl}$: } &-i\omega_{kl} \\
&\big\Updownarrow \\
\text{Eigenoperator of $\text{ad}_H$: } &c^\dagger_l c_k \text{ with eigenvalue } i\omega_{kl} \\
&\big\Updownarrow \\
\text{Excitation energy: } &E_l - E_k = \hbar\omega_{kl} \\
&\big\Updownarrow \\
\text{Energy levels of $H$: } &\{E_k\}
\end{aligned} \tag{43}
\]

The three-level chain above shows that the spectral content of $M$ (its eigenvalues $\pm i\omega_{kl}$ and eigenvectors $Z_{kl}$) is **identical** to the spectral content of $H$ (its energy differences $\{E_k - E_l\}$ and excitation operators $\{c^\dagger_l c_k\}$). They are the same physics expressed in different representations.

**"Category error":** The reviewer's accusation would be valid if the paper had claimed $M = H$ or $M$ acts on the same space as $H$. But the correct claim — which we now state rigorously — is:

> $M$ is the **dynamical manifestation** of $H$'s spectral structure in the correlation observable space. The non-diagonalizability of $M$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is the **observable-level proof** that $H$'s spectral structure — specifically, the independent single-particle excitation modes that underpin occupation numbers, Fock space, and quantum numbers — requires $\mathbb{C}$ for its consistent representation.

The "category" of $M$ is not "Hamiltonian" but **"real representation of the adjoint action of the Hamiltonian on the single-particle correlation sector."** Once this category is correctly identified, the spectral correspondence theorem bridges the gap, and the argument becomes rigorous.

## Section 8: Concrete Example — Two-Level System

We illustrate the bridge with the simplest non-trivial case: $N=2$ fermionic modes (or a two-level system).

### 8.1 The Setup

Hamiltonian: $H = E_1 c^\dagger_1 c_1 + E_2 c^\dagger_2 c_2$ with $E_1 \neq E_2$.

Single-particle Hamiltonian matrix:
\[
h = \begin{pmatrix} E_1 & 0 \\ 0 & E_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \omega_{12} = \frac{E_1 - E_2}{\hbar} \equiv \omega \neq 0. \tag{44}
\]

Correlation matrix:
\[
C(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \langle n_1(t) \rangle & \langle c^\dagger_2 c_1(t) \rangle \\ \langle c^\dagger_1 c_2(t) \rangle & \langle n_2(t) \rangle \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} n_1(t) & Z^*(t) \\ Z(t) & n_2(t) \end{pmatrix}, \tag{45}
\]
where $Z(t) = \langle c^\dagger_1 c_2(t) \rangle = C^R_{12}(t) + i C^I_{12}(t)$.

### 8.2 The Dynamics

From Eq.~(8):
\[
\frac{d}{dt} \begin{pmatrix} n_1 \\ Z \\ Z^* \\ n_2 \end{pmatrix}
= \frac{i}{\hbar} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & h_{12} & -h_{21} & 0 \\ h_{21} & E_2 - E_1 & 0 & -h_{12} \\ -h_{12} & 0 & E_1 - E_2 & h_{21} \\ 0 & -h_{21} & h_{12} & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} n_1 \\ Z \\ Z^* \\ n_2 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{46}
\]

In the eigenbasis ($h_{12} = 0$), this simplifies to:
\[
\frac{dn_1}{dt} = 0,\quad \frac{dn_2}{dt} = 0,\quad \frac{dZ}{dt} = i\omega Z. \tag{47}
\]

Occupation numbers $n_1, n_2$ are conserved ($[H, \hat{n}_k] = 0$ only in the eigenbasis because the Hamiltonian is diagonal). The coherence $Z$ oscillates at the Bohr frequency $\omega$.

### 8.3 The Real Representation and Its Failure

Write $Z = C^R + i C^I$. From (47):
\[
\frac{d}{dt} \begin{pmatrix} C^R \\ C^I \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega \\ \omega & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} C^R \\ C^I \end{pmatrix}
\equiv M \begin{pmatrix} C^R \\ C^I \end{pmatrix}. \tag{48}
\]

$M$ has eigenvalues $\pm i\omega$, no real eigenvectors. The real variables $C^R, C^I$ are permanently coupled.

### 8.4 The Bridge in This Case

Now, the occupation numbers $n_1, n_2$ are conserved in this eigenbasis. But the **physical meaning** of $n_1$ — "the number of particles in mode 1" — requires mode 1 to be an identifiable, independent degree of freedom. How do we know that mode 1 is independent? We look at the excitations involving mode 1 and mode 2: the coherence $C_{12}$. If $C_{12}$ evolves independently from $C_{21}$ and from $n_1, n_2$, then mode 1 and mode 2 are dynamically independent — the occupation of mode 1 can be varied without affecting mode 2.

In the real representation, $C^R_{12}$ and $C^I_{12}$ are permanently coupled. You cannot say "this real quantity evolves independently because it is the mode 1-mode 2 coherence." The coupling is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$.

In the complex representation, $Z_{12} = C^R_{12} + i C^I_{12}$ evolves as $dZ_{12}/dt = i\omega Z_{12}$ — a fully independent 1D complex equation. $Z_{12}$ IS the independent mode of coherence between levels 1 and 2.

**The occupation numbers $n_1, n_2$ are meaningful precisely because the coherence $Z_{12}$ has an independent complex representation.** Without this, the labels "mode 1" and "mode 2" are not dynamically justified — they are merely formal names for rows/columns of a matrix whose off-diagonal dynamics is intractably coupled.

### 8.5 What the Reviewer Might Say Next

**Anticipated counterobjection:** "But in this two-level case, $n_1$ and $n_2$ are conserved regardless of the coherence dynamics. The coherent coupling between $C^R$ and $C^I$ is irrelevant for the occupation numbers."

**Response:** This objection misses the point. The occupation numbers are conserved *only* in the eigenbasis. To define $n_1$ and $n_2$ as independent conserved quantities, you must first *find* the eigenbasis through diagonalization of $h$. The diagonalization of $h$ produces eigenvectors $|1\rangle, |2\rangle$ that are generally complex. Moreover, the coherence $Z_{12} = \langle c^\dagger_1 c_2 \rangle$ that connects them is intrinsically complex — its real representation $M$ fails to be diagonalizable. The occupation numbers are meaningful *as physical labels* only because the complex coherence $Z_{12}$ provides the independent dynamical channel that justifies treating modes 1 and 2 as separate entities.

If one works in a basis where occupation numbers are not conserved (e.g., a site basis with hopping), then the dynamics of $n_k$ is directly coupled to the coherences, and without the complex decoupling, the occupation numbers cannot be defined as independent labels.
